Designing a horizontal rail that’s easy to browse

When we redesigned our weekend trail guide, we needed room for more routes. The hardest part was making the next card easy to find.

Maya Chen September 17, 2026

Last spring, we added six short hikes to our weekend trail guide. On a laptop, the previews fit neatly into a grid. On a phone, the same grid became a long list that pushed the parking and weather notes several screens down. We moved those optional previews into a horizontal rail and kept the information people needed for their chosen hike in the main reading flow.

The first prototype showed one full card on a phone. During a walkthrough, a friend read the first preview and kept scrolling down the page. She was looking for a shorter route and assumed we had only included one. A second person found the extra cards by swiping the photo, then struggled to return to the first one. Both observations changed how we sized and aligned the cards.

Leave room for the next card

We sized the cards so the first one could show a route name, distance, and a short description with a sliver of the second photo still visible. The gap between cards mattered. If we made it too wide, the next image disappeared at 360 pixels. If we made it too narrow, the second image looked attached to the first card.

“Show enough of the next card that someone can tell where a swipe will take them.”

We checked the layout with the longest route name and with larger browser text. At both sizes, the trail name could wrap and the distance stayed visible. The photograph could get smaller because the name and distance did most of the work when someone was comparing routes.

Let the browser handle scrolling

We used agent-ui’s scroll row with an explicit item-width. The row handles horizontal overflow and card snapping. The rail has an accessible name from its heading, and tabindex="0" gives keyboard users a place to focus before using the arrow keys.

<h2 id="routes">More routes</h2>
<ui-row scroll item-width="17rem"
        gap="md" tabindex="0"
        role="region"
        aria-labelledby="routes">
  <ui-card>
    <h3>Creek Loop</h3>
    <p>2.4 miles · Easy</p>
  </ui-card>
  <ui-card>
    <h3>Lower Ridge</h3>
    <p>4.1 miles · Moderate</p>
  </ui-card>
</ui-row>

On a laptop, start at the heading and press Tab until the rail has focus. Arrow across to the final card and back again. Then try the links or controls inside the cards in order. On a phone, start a vertical swipe over a card and check that the page still scrolls naturally.

Make each card easy to compare

We kept the same order on every preview: photo, trail name, distance, and one detail worth knowing. For Creek Loop, that detail was shade for most of the walk. For Lower Ridge, it was a steep stretch near the end. Those specifics gave people a reason to pick one route over another.

After the change, we gave a reader a simple task: find a route under three miles with some shade. She found Creek Loop and went back to the original hike without losing her place. We kept that task for later reviews because it tests whether the cards help someone make a decision.
