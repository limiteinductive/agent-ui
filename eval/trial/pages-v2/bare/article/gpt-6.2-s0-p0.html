The quiet work of keeping a website small

What I learned from rebuilding a six-year-old personal site one page at a time.

Maya Chen
October 14, 2026

Last winter I opened my personal site to fix a broken link. By the time I found the right template, I had upgraded a package, chased a build warning, and forgotten which link I meant to fix. The site had twenty-three essays and a contact page. I wanted an edit to feel like opening a notebook and crossing out a line.

Start with one ordinary page

I picked an essay from the middle of the archive. It had a photograph, two headings, and a footnote. I wrote down what I expected a reader to do there: read the essay, follow a source, and find another piece if they felt like staying. That short list gave me somewhere to start when I opened the template.

The first thing I removed was a script that highlighted the current section in the sidebar. On a phone the sidebar was hidden, but the script still ran. I kept the section links at the top of longer essays, where readers could reach them on any screen.

“I wanted to be able to open last year’s essay, fix a sentence, and get on with my afternoon.”

Give images their dimensions

On the train home, I noticed that a paragraph jumped down just as I started reading it. The photograph above it had finally loaded. I added the image’s width and height to the markup so the browser could reserve space before the file arrived.

I also exported a smaller copy for narrow screens. The browser uses srcset and sizes to choose a candidate based on the space available and the screen’s pixel density. This is roughly what a photograph halfway down an essay looks like now.

<img
  src="/photos/ferry-960.jpg"
  srcset="/photos/ferry-480.jpg 480w,
          /photos/ferry-960.jpg 960w"
  sizes="(max-width: 42rem) calc(100vw - 2rem), 40rem"
  width="960"
  height="640"
  loading="lazy"
  alt="An evening ferry crossing the bay"
>

The same image rule scales each candidate to the column. I leave lazy loading off the cover because it’s usually visible as soon as the article opens.

Read an entire draft on your phone

Desktop previews made the redesign look finished sooner than it was. On my phone, an old code block pushed the whole page sideways, and a caption wrapped into five cramped lines. Reading a few paragraphs at a time had missed both. I found them when I read the essay from the byline to the last footnote.

I gave wide code samples their own horizontal scroll and shortened the caption to the detail I wanted readers to notice. Then I worked through the archive in batches of three, checking one older photo-heavy essay and one newer text-heavy essay with each change.

A month later

A reader emailed me about a typo in an old piece on bus timetables. I found the essay, changed two words, and published the correction before my coffee went cold. I still have a list of things I’d like to change. That afternoon, I got to do the one I came for.
