Designing trail maps for tired eyes

What a rainy walk taught us about drawing a map people can read in a few seconds.

Maya Chen October 8, 2026

At the last fork before the ridge, I pulled out my phone and discovered that three dashed lines met at the same spot. One was the route we’d planned. One was an old service road. The third was a creek. With rain on the screen and my glasses fogging up, I could barely tell them apart.

Start at the fork

Back at my desk, I opened the map at the same zoom level and gave myself five seconds to pick a direction. I spent most of that time looking for the route line. The basemap had a dozen shades of green, and our thin blue line disappeared where it crossed a patch of dark forest.

I thickened the route and added a light outline so it would hold up over both trees and bare ground. At junctions, I kept the first visible stretch of each connecting trail just prominent enough to show which way it turned. Then I printed the map in grayscale. Two of the creek crossings still needed work, but I could finally follow the main route with my thumb.

“The map has to answer the question you’re standing still to ask.”

Name what people can see

My first draft labeled the summit and the trailhead. On the walk, we talked about the footbridge, the bend in the creek, and the split boulder beside the fork. Those were the places we could recognize from the trail, and they gave us something to check between turns.

At the next review, we looked at each label at the zoom level a walker would actually use. The footbridge stayed visible because it confirmed the crossing. The summit label moved to the overview, where it helped explain the shape of the whole walk. I left the split boulder out of the printed map until we could confirm that it was visible from both directions.

Show when a position is uncertain

Near the creek, the location marker jumped between banks. We already had an accuracy estimate from the browser, so I used it to change what the map drew. For this prototype, a fix with an estimated error above 25 meters gets a shaded area and a text label. Twenty-five meters was enough to cover both branches at our test junction.

function locationMarker(position) {
  const { latitude, longitude, accuracy } =
    position.coords;
  const uncertain = accuracy > 25;

  return {
    center: [longitude, latitude],
    radiusMeters: uncertain ? accuracy : 0,
    label: uncertain
      ? "Approximate location"
      : "Current location"
  };
}

The map uses that radius to draw the shaded area around the reported coordinates. We also removed the animation that snapped the marker to the closest trail. On the creek bank, it had made a noisy reading look like a confident instruction to cross.

Walk it again

We returned a week later and handed the revised map to someone who hadn’t seen the first draft. At each junction, we asked her which way she was going and what on the map told her so. At the creek she pointed to the footbridge, looked up, and saw it through the trees. At the final fork she paused. From that side, the sign was hidden behind a trunk.

That last pause gave us our next edit. We moved the fork label away from the route line and added a short description of the uphill branch. The following morning, I tried the map again with my phone at half brightness and one hand in my pocket. I could read the turn before I reached the sign.
